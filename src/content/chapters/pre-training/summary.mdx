Pre-training is best understood as a chain of decisions, not a single algorithm. Data supplies raw evidence. An objective turns that evidence into targets. An architecture controls which evidence the model may use. Adaptation connects the learned representation to a useful output.

<h2 id="mental-model">The chapter in one mental model</h2>

$$
\text{raw text} \longrightarrow \text{self-supervised target} \longrightarrow \hat{\theta} \longrightarrow \text{task adaptation}
$$

The first arrow creates supervision without manual labels. The second optimizes a general neural backbone. The last arrow specializes what was learned without repeating the entire expensive training process.

The essential distinctions are:

- **Supervised** targets come from an external labeler; **self-supervised** targets come from the data.
- **Decoder-only** models naturally continue sequences; **encoder-only** models create bidirectional representations; **encoder-decoder** models transform one sequence into another.
- **Feature extraction** freezes the backbone; **fine-tuning** moves some or all of its parameters.

<h2 id="decision-guide">A compact decision guide</h2>

| If you need to… | Begin with… | Then adapt by… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Generate an open-ended continuation | Decoder-only pre-training | Prompting or generative fine-tuning |
| Classify, tag, or retrieve text | Encoder-only pre-training | A task head plus selective fine-tuning |
| Convert an input into a structured output | Encoder-decoder pre-training | Conditional generation fine-tuning |

These are starting points, not laws. Large generative models can classify through prompting, and encoders can participate in generative systems. The table captures each architecture’s most direct fit.

<Callout title="A durable test">
  <p>When you encounter a new training method, ask three questions: What information is hidden? What must be predicted? Which parameters are allowed to change?</p>
</Callout>
