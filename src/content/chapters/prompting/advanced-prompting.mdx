Advanced prompting allocates additional information or inference-time computation to a request. The useful patterns are ways to expose a problem’s structure, generate alternatives, check a draft, or connect the model to evidence and capabilities outside its parameters.

<Callout title="Learning objectives">
  <ul>
    <li>Recognize when explicit reasoning and decomposition can help.</li>
    <li>Design refinement and ensemble loops with checkable evaluators.</li>
    <li>Connect retrieval and tools without treating their outputs as trusted instructions.</li>
  </ul>
</Callout>

<h2 id="chain-of-thought">Chain-of-thought prompting</h2>

Chain-of-thought prompting asks the model to produce intermediate reasoning before its conclusion. A few-shot prompt can demonstrate the desired reasoning pattern, while a zero-shot prompt can request a deliberate solution. This often helps tasks containing several dependent operations because the model receives more tokens over which to represent the computation.

The technique also has costs. Longer outputs increase latency, may expose irrelevant internal text, and can present a persuasive explanation for an incorrect result. Reasoning text should be treated as a candidate derivation, not proof. High-stakes answers still need executable checks, source evidence, or an independent verifier.

<Callout title="Reasoning text is not proof">
  <p>Fluency measures how plausible an explanation sounds. Verification measures whether its claims and operations are correct. Keep those two judgments separate.</p>
</Callout>

<h2 id="problem-decomposition">Problem decomposition</h2>

Decomposition breaks a difficult request into smaller subproblems. In a sequential design, the output of one step becomes the input to the next. In a parallel design, independent subtasks are solved separately and combined. Least-to-most prompting begins with simpler prerequisites and progressively constructs the final answer.

Good subproblems have explicit inputs, bounded outputs, and a way to detect failure. This makes the workflow observable and allows a failed step to be retried. Poor decomposition simply spreads the original ambiguity across more model calls. It can also propagate an early error, so preserve source evidence and validate intermediate results at important boundaries.

A useful design test is whether each step could be assigned and checked independently. If not, the split may be procedural theater rather than a meaningful reduction in complexity.

<h2 id="self-refinement">Self-refinement</h2>

Self-refinement uses a loop such as **draft, critique, revise**. The critique should name defects against explicit criteria, and the revision should address those defects without discarding correct material. Separating the roles into different prompts or model calls can make the state transitions clearer.

Refinement works best when quality is locally recognizable: missing requirements, unsupported claims, invalid code, or inconsistent formatting. It is less reliable when the same model cannot detect its own misconception. Limit the number of iterations, retain the best verified candidate, and stop when measured improvement plateaus.

<h2 id="ensembling">Ensembling</h2>

Ensembling samples multiple candidate responses and aggregates them by majority vote, confidence-weighted voting, ranking, or a separate judge. Self-consistency applies this idea to reasoning paths: generate varied solutions and choose the answer supported by the strongest agreement.

The benefit depends on diversity and evaluator quality. Five nearly identical samples provide little independent evidence. Majority agreement can also amplify a shared bias, while a model judge may prefer polished language over correctness. Use task-specific checks whenever possible and compare the accuracy gain with the additional token and latency cost.

<h2 id="retrieval-and-tools">Retrieval and tools</h2>

Retrieval-augmented generation searches an external collection, ranks candidate passages, places selected evidence in the context, and asks the model to answer from that evidence. Failures can enter through indexing, query construction, ranking, context assembly, or generation. Citations help users inspect grounding, but citation presence alone does not prove that a claim follows from the cited passage.

Tool use extends the workflow: the model selects a function, produces arguments, receives a result, and continues. Calculators, databases, search systems, and business APIs provide exact or current capabilities that a model should not imitate from memory.

Applications must validate tool names and arguments, restrict permissions, time out failed operations, and distinguish untrusted results from system instructions. Retrieval text and tool output are data at a security boundary. They can inform the answer but must not silently redefine what the application is allowed to do.
