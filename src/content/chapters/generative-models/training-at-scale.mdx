Training at scale coordinates four coupled resources: data, parameters, arithmetic, and communication. Increasing one while neglecting the others produces an expensive bottleneck rather than a better model.

<h2 id="data-preparation">Data preparation</h2>

Raw web text is abundant but not automatically suitable for training. A practical pipeline typically performs:

1. **Extraction and normalization** to recover readable text and consistent encodings.
2. **Language and quality filtering** to remove malformed or low-information documents.
3. **Deduplication** at exact and near-duplicate levels.
4. **Safety, privacy, and policy filtering** for content that should not enter training.
5. **Mixture weighting** across domains, languages, code, books, papers, and conversational sources.
6. **Tokenization and packing** into training sequences with minimal padding.

Deduplication matters because repeated documents distort the effective distribution, waste compute, and increase memorization risk. Aggressive filtering can remove most scraped data, but the filter itself introduces preferences about language, style, and quality.

The corpus mixture is a model parameter in everything but name. Upweighting code, mathematics, or a low-resource language changes capabilities and trade-offs even if architecture and token budget remain fixed.

<h2 id="model-modifications">Model modifications</h2>

Large decoder architectures keep the Transformer core but adjust components for stability and efficiency.

- **Pre-normalization** applies layer normalization before attention or the feed-forward network, improving gradient flow through deep residual stacks.
- **RMS-style normalization** normalizes magnitude with less computation than full mean-and-variance normalization.
- **Gated feed-forward networks** combine an activation gate with a value projection and often improve parameter use.
- **Rotary or relative positions** encode token offsets in attention and support later context extension.
- **Grouped or multi-query attention** reduces the number of key/value heads, lowering inference-cache memory.

For a standard feed-forward layer,

$$
\operatorname{FFN}(h)
=
\sigma(hW_1+b_1)W_2+b_2
$$

the hidden dimension is usually several times larger than model width. This sublayer therefore contains a large fraction of parameters and compute. Gating can improve expressiveness, but larger hidden dimensions increase both training and serving cost.

Architecture changes must be evaluated as a system. A modification that stabilizes training may affect kernel efficiency; a cache-saving attention design changes inference economics even if pre-training cost is similar.

<h2 id="distributed-training">Distributed training</h2>

No single accelerator can hold the largest model, optimizer state, activations, and batch. Distributed training divides them along several axes.

| Strategy | What is divided | Main communication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Data parallelism | Minibatch examples | Gradient synchronization |
| Tensor parallelism | Matrix operations within layers | Partial activations/results |
| Pipeline parallelism | Consecutive layer groups | Activations between stages |
| State sharding | Parameters, gradients, optimizer state | Gather/reduce as needed |

In data parallelism, worker $k$ computes a gradient on shard $D_k$ and workers aggregate:

$$
g
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}
\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_\theta(D_k)
$$

Tensor parallelism fits wide layers across devices. Pipeline parallelism fits deep stacks but creates bubbles when stages wait. State sharding reduces redundant memory. Large systems combine all three, making topology and collective communication as important as nominal device count.

Low-precision training reduces memory traffic and accelerates matrix multiplication. Master weights, loss scaling, or carefully chosen formats preserve enough dynamic range for stable updates. Checkpointing activations trades extra recomputation for lower memory.

The useful metric is not peak hardware throughput; it is sustained training progress per unit time and cost. Data loading, synchronization, stragglers, failures, and checkpoint writes all reduce utilization.

<h2 id="scaling-laws">Scaling laws</h2>

Empirical scaling laws approximate validation loss as a power law of model parameters $N$, dataset size $D$, or compute $C$:

$$
\mathcal{L}(N)\approx aN^{-\alpha}+b,
\qquad
\mathcal{L}(D)\approx cD^{-\beta}+d
$$

Power laws are valuable because small runs can forecast larger ones within an observed regime. They also reveal bottlenecks: adding parameters yields diminishing benefit if the model sees too few tokens, while feeding enormous data to insufficient capacity eventually saturates.

**Compute-optimal training** chooses $N$ and $D$ together for a fixed compute budget. Deployment can change the optimum. If inference happens billions of times, a smaller model trained on more tokens may cost more to train but far less to serve.

Scaling-law limitations are equally important:

- coefficients depend on architecture, data, tokenizer, and metric;
- downstream task trends can differ from pre-training loss;
- a smooth average does not predict a specific emergent capability;
- distribution shifts and data quality can break extrapolation.

Use scaling laws to allocate experiments and budgets, then validate the resulting model on the actual capability and risk suite.
