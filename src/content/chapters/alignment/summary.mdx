Alignment adds targeted behavioral signals after broad pre-training. Demonstrations teach a basic response policy; comparisons express relative preferences; reward-based or direct objectives push the model toward preferred behavior.

<h2 id="mental-model">The chapter in one mental model</h2>

$$
\text{pre-trained model}
\xrightarrow{\text{demonstrations}} \text{instruction policy}
\xrightarrow{\text{preferences}} \text{aligned policy}
$$

The second step may use a reward model and reinforcement learning, direct preference optimization, or selected synthetic feedback. Runtime verification can further shape the response without changing model weights.

<h2 id="method-guide">Method guide</h2>

| Available signal | Common method | Central limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ideal responses | Supervised fine-tuning | Imitates demonstrations only |
| Chosen and rejected responses | RLHF | Reward-model and RL complexity |
| Preference pairs with a reference | DPO | Sensitive to data and reference policy |
| Written principles or model critiques | Automatic feedback | Evaluator blind spots can compound |
| Verifiable intermediate steps | Process supervision | More labeling and specification effort |
| Runtime checks and candidate scores | Inference-time alignment | Additional serving cost and latency |

<h2 id="failure-modes">Failure modes to track</h2>

Watch for reward hacking, superficial preference shortcuts, loss of useful capabilities, excessive refusal, inconsistent behavior across groups, evaluator disagreement, and performance that collapses under distribution shift. A rising training reward is not enough; use independent task metrics, human review, adversarial testing, and post-deployment monitoring.

Alignment objectives are surrogates for the behavior a deployment actually needs. Their assumptions and tradeoffs should remain visible.
