Instruction alignment begins with supervised fine-tuning (SFT): continue training a pre-trained language model on examples of instructions paired with desired responses. It uses familiar next-token learning on a deliberately constructed interaction distribution.

<h2 id="supervised-fine-tuning">Supervised fine-tuning</h2>

For an instruction or conversation $x$ and a target response $y=(y_1,\ldots,y_T)$, SFT minimizes the response-token loss

$$
\mathcal{L}_{SFT} = -\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log P_\theta(y_t \mid x,y_{<t}).
$$

The prompt tokens provide context, while the loss is commonly masked so that optimization focuses on the assistant response. Multi-turn examples teach role boundaries, references to earlier messages, corrections, and follow-up behavior.

SFT is stable, efficient, and gives preference optimization a useful starting policy. It also has an imitation ceiling: the model is trained to reproduce target responses, not to compare all possible answers or directly maximize a human preference.

<h2 id="instruction-data">Instruction data</h2>

Instruction examples can be written by experts, adapted from existing NLP tasks, collected from real interactions with privacy controls, or generated by a stronger model. A useful mixture spans task families, difficulty levels, domains, formats, conversational turns, uncertainty, and appropriate refusals.

Human-written data offers control but is expensive. Transformed benchmarks add coverage but may feel artificial. Synthetic generation scales cheaply and can diversify instructions, yet it can copy the teacher’s factual mistakes, stylistic habits, and safety gaps. Filtering, deduplication, source tracking, and human review determine whether volume becomes useful supervision.

<h2 id="less-data">Learning with less data</h2>

Large pre-trained models already contain language and task knowledge, so instruction tuning often needs far fewer examples than pre-training. Carefully selected, diverse demonstrations can teach the interaction pattern more effectively than a large repetitive collection.

Data efficiency methods prioritize high-quality seeds, generate variations, select examples that cover new skills, and remove redundant or low-confidence items. The goal is not the smallest dataset in isolation; it is the best retained and new-task performance for the labeling and training budget.

<h2 id="instruction-generalization">Instruction generalization</h2>

An instruction-following model should infer the task from unfamiliar wording rather than memorize training prompts. Generalization improves when training covers many task structures and expresses consistent principles across them. Evaluation should hold out task families, formats, and domains, not merely paraphrases of seen examples.

Conflicting demonstrations weaken the inferred policy. Clear role formatting, balanced coverage, and examples of edge behavior help the model learn a reusable mapping from intent to response.

<h2 id="weak-to-strong">Weak-to-strong supervision</h2>

As models become capable in domains that human supervisors cannot fully solve, direct labels may be unreliable. Weak-to-strong supervision asks whether feedback from a weaker judge can still improve a stronger model. Decomposition, debate, tool-assisted checking, process supervision, and verifiable subtasks attempt to make difficult outputs easier to evaluate.

The core risk is that the stronger policy learns to satisfy the weak judge rather than solve the real task. Use independent tests, ground-truth subsets, adversarial examples, and calibrated uncertainty to reveal that gap.
