Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) turns comparisons between model responses into a learned reward, then updates the language model to receive more of that reward while remaining close to a capable reference policy.

<h2 id="reinforcement-learning">Reinforcement learning basics</h2>

An RL problem contains states, actions, rewards, and a policy. For language generation, the prompt and generated prefix form the state, the next token is an action, and the language model is the policy $\pi_\theta$. A scalar reward is usually assigned after the response is complete.

Policy optimization increases the probability of sampled actions associated with high return. Unlike SFT, it does not require a target token for every position. It can therefore optimize sequence-level qualities, but its estimates are noisy and its policy changes can be unstable.

In RLHF, the environment is largely the prompt distribution and learned scorer. The scorer is only a proxy for human judgment, so reward improvement must be checked against independent evaluation.

<h2 id="reward-model">Reward model</h2>

Annotators compare candidate responses for the same prompt, producing a chosen response $y^+$ and rejected response $y^-$. Pairwise judgments are often easier than assigning reliable absolute scores. A reward model $r_\phi(x,y)$ is trained to score the chosen response higher, commonly with

$$
\mathcal{L}_{RM} = -\log \sigma\left(r_\phi(x,y^+) - r_\phi(x,y^-)\right).
$$

Collection guidelines must define whether preference combines correctness, helpfulness, safety, style, or separate dimensions. Candidate generation should expose meaningful differences, and disagreement should be measured rather than silently converted into certainty.

The reward model learns patterns in limited comparisons. It may mistake length, confidence, or familiar phrasing for quality. Held-out human judgments, calibration checks, and adversarial candidates help expose these shortcuts.

<h2 id="policy-optimization">Training the language model</h2>

The policy generates responses, the reward model scores them, and an RL algorithm updates the policy. A typical objective combines predicted reward with a penalty for divergence from a reference model:

$$
\max_\theta\; \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[r_\phi(x,y)]
- \beta D_{KL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{ref}).
$$

The KL term limits drift so that optimization does not discard fluency, diversity, or broad capabilities in pursuit of the learned score. The coefficient $\beta$ controls the strength of that anchor.

RLHF is operationally demanding: it interleaves generation, scoring, advantage estimation, policy updates, and stability controls. Over-optimization can exploit weaknesses in the reward model, so teams track both reward and independent human or task-specific metrics throughout training.
