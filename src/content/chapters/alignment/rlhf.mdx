Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) has two main parts:

1. It changes comparisons between model responses into a learned reward.
2. It updates the language model to get more of this reward. The model must also stay near a capable reference policy.

<h2 id="reinforcement-learning">Reinforcement learning basics</h2>

A reinforcement learning (RL) problem contains states, actions, rewards and a policy. For language generation:

- The **state** is the prompt and the generated prefix.
- An **action** is the next token.
- The **policy** $\pi_\theta$ is the language model.
- The **reward** is usually one number that the model gets after the response is complete.

Policy optimization increases the probability of sampled actions that get a high return. SFT needs a target token at each position, but RL does not. Thus, RL can optimize qualities of the full sequence. But its estimates have much noise, and its policy changes can be unstable.

In RLHF, the environment is mostly the prompt distribution and the learned scorer. The scorer is only a proxy for human judgment. Thus, you must compare the reward improvement with an independent evaluation.

<h2 id="reward-model">Training reward models</h2>

Annotators compare candidate responses for the same prompt. They select a chosen response $y^+$ and a rejected response $y^-$. A comparison of two responses is often easier than a reliable absolute score. A reward model $r_\phi(x,y)$ learns to give the chosen response a higher score. Usually, it uses this loss:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{RM} = -\log \sigma\left(r_\phi(x,y^+) - r_\phi(x,y^-)\right).
$$

The loss is small when the reward of the chosen response is much higher than the reward of the rejected response.

The collection guidelines must define what "preference" means. It can combine correctness, helpfulness, safety and style, or it can keep them as separate dimensions. The candidate generation must show differences that are useful. Measure the disagreement between annotators. Do not hide it and show it as certainty.

The reward model learns patterns from a limited number of comparisons. It can think that length, confidence or familiar wording is quality. Held-out human judgments, calibration checks and hostile candidates help find these shortcuts.

<h2 id="policy-optimization">Training the language model</h2>

The training loop has three steps:

1. The policy generates responses.
2. The reward model gives them scores.
3. An RL algorithm updates the policy.

A usual objective combines the predicted reward with a penalty for distance from a reference model:

$$
\max_\theta\; \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[r_\phi(x,y)]
- \beta D_{KL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{ref}).
$$

The policy tries to get a high average reward. The second term subtracts a penalty when the policy becomes too different from the reference model.

The KL term gives a penalty when the policy moves away from the reference. This helps decrease the risk that the optimization loses fluency, diversity or general skills to get a higher learned score. It does not guarantee it. The coefficient $\beta$ controls how strong this penalty is.

RLHF is difficult to operate. It mixes generation, scoring, advantage estimation, policy updates and stability controls.

> **Caution:** Too much optimization can exploit weaknesses in the reward model. Thus, during all of the training, monitor the reward and also independent human or task-specific metrics.
